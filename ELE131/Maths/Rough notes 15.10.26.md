Differentiation tells us how quickly a quantity is accumulating, while integration tells us how much of that quantity has accumulated.

The derivative $\frac{dy}{dx}$ or f'(x) measures how quickly $y$ changes as $x$ changes. Geometrically, this is the tangent to a graph. Physically, this is a rate of change, and in engineering it shows the sensitivity of one quantity given a change in another.

E.g. $I(t)=\frac{dq}{dt}$ (current is rate of flow of charge)

Remember if $y=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ then the quotient rule is $\frac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{g(x)^2}$.

This can be more easily remembered as: $\frac{\text{Bottom}\times\text{derivative of top}-\text{Top}\times\text{derivative of bottom}}{\text{bottom}^{2}}$ 

If $y=f(u)$ where $u=g(x)$ (so effectively $y=f(g(x))$) then remember $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}$.

If $x=a$ is a stationary point on the graph $y=f(x)$, then $f(a)=0$. If $f''(a)>0$, this is a local maximum, and if $f''(a)<0$ then this is a local minimum. If $f''(a)=0$ then the tests is inconclusive and further analysis is needed.
